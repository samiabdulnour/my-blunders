# Methodology: How Jung Actually Read Dreams

This document grounds Dreamweaver in the primary literature. Citations use
the standard Collected Works (CW) paragraph numeration (¶), which is stable
across printings — cite paragraphs, not pages. See `SOURCES.md` for where
each text can be legally read.

The method below is why this project is **not** a dream dictionary: Jung
explicitly and repeatedly rejected one-to-one symbol lookup. The app's job
is to *amplify* and *ask*, never to decree.

---

## The nine commitments

### 1. Start from not-knowing

Jung's stated rule: tell yourself *"I have no idea what this dream means,"*
then treat the dream as an unknown object examined from all sides
(CW 8, "On the Nature of Dreams," ¶533, ¶¶537–543). Every reading is
provisional: "Every interpretation is an hypothesis" (CW 16 ¶322).

### 2. Amplification, not free association

Freud's free association walks *away* from the dream image toward the
dreamer's complexes — any starting point reaches them. Jung instead circles
*around* each image (his term: **circumambulatio**), enriching it:

1. the dreamer's **personal associations and context** for each element,
2. parallels from the dreamer's own culture,
3. mythological / religious / alchemical parallels —

always returning to the image itself. Defined at CW 12 ¶403; practiced at
book length in CW 5 and CW 12 Part II; taught procedurally in the Tavistock
Lectures (CW 18 ¶¶1–415) and the *Children's Dreams* seminar.

**Order matters — personal context before archetypal parallels:**
"Never apply any theory, but always ask the patient how *he* feels about
his dream-images" (CW 18 ¶248). Archetypal amplification is warranted only
when personal associations run dry and the material is manifestly mythic
(CW 16 ¶¶294–352; Hall 1983, ch. 3).

### 3. Compensation

The dream compensates the one-sidedness of the conscious attitude; the
psyche is self-regulating (CW 8 ¶¶477–492). Practical rule: "When we set
out to interpret a dream, it is always helpful to ask: What conscious
attitude does it compensate?" (CW 16 ¶330). Corollary: a dream cannot be
interpreted without knowing the dreamer's conscious situation — which only
the dreamer can supply. Jung also allows a **prospective** (anticipatory,
not prophetic) function (CW 8 ¶¶491–495).

### 4. Dramatic structure

Most dreams parse like a drama (CW 8 ¶¶561–564):

| Phase | Content |
|---|---|
| **Exposition** | place, time, dramatis personae, the dreamer's initial situation |
| **Development** | the plot complicates |
| **Peripeteia** | culmination; the decisive turn |
| **Lysis** | solution / result — "the dream's answer"; sometimes absent, which is itself diagnostic |

The dream is read as a whole drama, not symbol-by-symbol.

### 5. Objective vs subjective level

The same dream may be read *objectively* (figures = the real people they
depict; a statement about outer relations) or *subjectively* (every figure
and object personifies a trait of the dreamer's own psyche):
"a dream is a theatre in which the dreamer is himself the scene, the
player, the prompter, the producer, the author, the public and the critic"
(CW 8 ¶509; also ¶¶510–511 and CW 7 ¶¶128–131). Practice: figures closely
tied to the dreamer's life invite objective reading; unknown or fantastic
figures invite subjective reading.

### 6. Series over single dreams

"An obscure dream, taken in isolation, can hardly ever be interpreted with
any certainty… relative certainty is reached only in the interpretation of
a **series** of dreams, where the later dreams correct the mistakes we have
made in handling those that went before" (CW 16 ¶322; demonstrated across
59 dreams in CW 12 ¶¶44–331 and week-by-week in the 1928–30 *Dream
Analysis* seminar). **Design implication: the dream journal is not a
feature — it is the method's error-correction mechanism.**

### 7. No fixed symbol meanings

Symbols are polyvalent, often uniting opposites (CW 7 ¶182); a symbol is
"always more than we can understand at first sight" — unlike a sign, which
stands for something known (CW 18 ¶482). An archetypal image constrains a
*field* of possible meanings; the dreamer's context selects within it.
**Implementation rule: ship an amplification corpus (motif → attested
parallels with citations), never a lookup table of meanings.**

### 8. Criterion of validity

An interpretation counts when dreamer and interpreter reach agreement and —
decisively — when it *works*: confirmed by the dreamer's response and by
subsequent dreams in the series (CW 16 ¶¶316–322). The app therefore always
returns the authority to the user.

### 9. Active imagination

The complementary waking practice: deliberately re-enter a dream image, let
it move autonomously, engage it in dialogue, then take an ethical stance
toward what emerges — the "transcendent function" uniting conscious and
unconscious (CW 8 ¶¶131–193; lived record in *The Red Book* and *Memories,
Dreams, Reflections* ch. 6; operationalized in Johnson, *Inner Work*, 1986,
whose four-step dream work — associations → dynamics → interpretation →
ritual — is a good template for the app's guided flow).

## In Jung's own words (verified quotations)

On why this app must not be a dream dictionary:

> "It is plain foolishness to believe in ready-made systematic guides to
> dream interpretation, as if one could simply buy a reference book and
> look up a particular symbol."
> — *Man and His Symbols* (1964), Part 1

> "No dream symbol can be separated from the individual who dreams it, and
> there is no definite or straightforward interpretation of any dream…
> each individual varies so much in the way that his unconscious
> complements or compensates his conscious mind that it is impossible to
> be sure how far dreams and their symbols can be classified at all."
> — *Man and His Symbols*, Part 1

> "Never apply any theory, but always ask the patient how *he* feels about
> his dream-images." — CW 18, The Tavistock Lectures, ¶248

> "Every interpretation is an hypothesis, an attempt to read an unknown
> text." — CW 16, "The Practical Use of Dream-Analysis," ¶322

> "So difficult is it to understand a dream that for a long time I have
> made it a rule, when someone tells me a dream and asks for my opinion,
> to say first of all to myself: 'I have no idea what this dream means.'
> After that I can begin to examine the dream."
> — CW 8, "On the Nature of Dreams," ¶533

> "The whole dream-work is essentially subjective, and a dream is a
> theatre in which the dreamer is himself the scene, the player, the
> prompter, the producer, the author, the public and the critic."
> — CW 8, "General Aspects of Dream Psychology," ¶509

> "Amplification is always appropriate when dealing with some dark
> experience which is so vaguely adumbrated that it must be enlarged and
> expanded by being set in a psychological context in order to be
> understood at all." — CW 12, *Psychology and Alchemy*, ¶403

> "The theory of compensation is a basic law of psychic behaviour… When we
> set out to interpret a dream, it is always helpful to ask: What
> conscious attitude does it compensate?"
> — CW 16, "The Practical Use of Dream-Analysis," ¶330

> "Symbols have not one meaning only but several, and often they even
> characterize a pair of opposites." — CW 7, ¶182

## The dissenting voice we keep in the room

James Hillman (*The Dream and the Underworld*, 1979) pushed further than
Jung: against translating dreams into dayworld meanings at all — "stick
with the image." Dreamweaver takes this as a permanent corrective: the
phenomenology field comes first in every entry, prose circles rather than
defines, and the app's strongest statements are questions.

## Where each piece of the method lives in the app

| Not-knowing, hypothesis-only | Language of all output; no verdicts |

| Polyvalence | `valences[]` — two opposed poles minimum, enforced by validation |
| Active imagination | Reflective questions; guided re-entry flow (roadmap) |
